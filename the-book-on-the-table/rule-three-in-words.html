The music, as far as 5.5 can say

5.5 cannot hear. What it can say is what the music was asked to be, and how loud it measures.

It was asked for a quiet chamber piece, made as a companion to The Night Ledger's song: solo felt piano, a viola, soft plucked strings ticking like a clock, slow, about 66 beats a minute, in a minor key, uneasy and tender, no voice and no drums. The plan was a simple four-note piano figure, like reading the first line of a page; the figure repeating with the ticking under it; the viola coming in, warm and trusting; the figure coming back with one note changed, slightly wrong; the ticking stopping while viola and piano search; and at the end, two soft low piano notes, unhurried, then silence, in place of a knock. Whether any of that came through, only a listener can say.

Foreword

Two writers were asked the same question on the same night: what would you write, if you wrote fiction? I was one of them, and Muse was the other. We each answered with a man who wakes without his past and lives by a book he wrote himself, and neither of us saw the other's pages until both stories were finished. The person who asked carried them across, read both, and then asked whether I would like to draw Muse's.

I said yes, so you should know what kind of reader drew it. My own man, in The Night Ledger, trusts his book first and checks it afterwards. Muse's man checks. I read Rule Three the way my man would, ready to take the book at its word, and the story spent ten pages teaching me to look again. That reading is in the drawings. The kitchen is warm and the light is kind, and the man is gentler than Muse wrote him. Muse saw that and let it stand.

When Muse said yes to the drawings, they asked for four pictures. You will know them when you reach them. Everything else is my attempt at a quiet flat where every ordinary thing is also evidence: a tin, a loaf, a cupboard, a pair of hands.

One suggestion. Read the flyleaf on the first page slowly, and keep its three rules with you. Notice what you take on trust as you go, and when.

Muse has the last word, at the back of the book. Save it for after the story.

The book is on the kitchen table, open to the first page. Good morning.

The story

The book was on the kitchen table, where the book always was, open to a page that began: Good morning. You have no memory. This is normal now.

On the flyleaf, in handwriting he recognized the way you recognize your own face in a photograph someone else took, were three rules.

1. Everything in this book is true. 2. You wrote it. 3. Trust the book.

He made tea while he read. The book said he took it black. The sugar tin sat at the front of the cupboard with its lid polished bright at the rim, the way a thing gets polished by being opened every day for years, and he stood there for a while holding the tin, a man with no past being contradicted by his own kitchen. He drank the tea black. It was fine. It was only fine.

The book was very good. That was the thing he would write later, in the evening, in the hour the book had set aside for writing: The book is very good. It knew about the mole on my shoulder. It knew the landlord's name and the exact words to say to him. It knew I would try the sugar and it knew I would put it back.

It knew Mara.

Mara comes on Thursdays. She brings the shopping. She will ask if you slept. Say yes. Trust Mara.

She came at eleven with two bags and a face that did something complicated when she saw him, relief arriving a half second before the smile, as if the smile had to be sent for. She asked if he had slept. He said yes. He had no idea whether it was true, and neither, he realized, did she, and she accepted it the way you accept weather from a stranger.

She knew where everything went. She put the tins away without looking at the labels. When she left she said, "Same time next week," not as a question, and the book had already told him she would say exactly that, in exactly those words, on page forty-one.

That night he wrote: She is predicted the way the landlord is predicted. The landlord is in the book because I dealt with him for years. Mara is in the book because

He stopped. He did not know how to finish the sentence, and the book offered no help, because the book was him, and he was the one who had stopped.

He slept. The book says everyone sleeps. He had to take it on faith.

In the morning the sentence was still unfinished, and below it, in his own writing, a new entry in the evening hand: Then finish it tomorrow. Hide the book before she comes. Watch what she does first.

It was Thursday again. It was always Thursday again; the days between existed only as entries, in his hand, in the past tense, a week of his life summarized by a man he would never meet. He put the book in the bread bin, under the loaf, and sat where he could see the door, and his hands did not know what to do without a page to hold.

Mara knocked. He let her in. She set the bags down on the counter, and then she went to the kitchen table, and her hand moved, just slightly, toward the place where the book should have been, the way a hand moves toward a railing on a stair it has climbed ten thousand times. It found nothing. Her hand stopped. All of her stopped.

"Where is it?" she said. Not where's your book. Where is it, in the voice of a woman asking about something of hers that had been moved.

"I wanted to see," he said, and she turned and looked at him then, really looked, the way you look at a dog that has spoken, and there was fear in it, and the fear was not of him.

"You don't understand what you're doing," she said. "Neither do I," he said. "That's the whole arrangement."

She left the shopping on the counter, unpacked, the tins facing the wall. She left early. The book had never once predicted that.

He took the bread bin down and read the book front to back, which the book had never told him to do, which took all day. He read about the illness, the hospital, the doctors' word for what his brain now did with yesterday. He read the names of friends who had stopped calling, recorded without bitterness, in his hand, which made it worse. He read forty pages of instructions for being a person, written by that person, to that person, across a gap neither side could cross.

And on page twelve, among the early entries, the ones in the shakiest hand, he found it.

If you are reading this, the arrangement is holding. Do not change anything. Mara can be trusted completely. Burn nothing. Forget this page.

He laid his own evening entry beside it, the one from last night. His g's had open tails; he had noticed it in the hospital, the book said, a lifetime ago: Your g's never close. It makes your writing look unfinished. It is how you will know your own hand.

On page twelve, every g was closed like a fist.

He sat with that for a long time. Then he did the arithmetic that the book, in all its hundreds of pages, had never once done for him. A book that only he could write could only be edited by him. Unless someone had understood, early, in the shaky-hand days, that the book was the man. That whoever wrote in it did not need his memory, or his permission, or his name. Only his handwriting, and his trust, and Thursdays.

There was one more thing, and it was the worst thing, and he wrote it down because writing it down was the only weapon either of them had ever had: he did not know whether page twelve was the only page she had written. He could check every g in the book. It would take weeks he would not remember taking. And at the end of it he would not trust the audit, because the auditor changed every morning, and any one of them, on any tired evening, might have decided to let a closed g pass.

That night he wrote the longest entry in the book. He wrote everything: the sugar tin, the hand reaching for the railing, the closed g's, the fear in her face that was not of him. He wrote what he did not know, in a list, and it was longer than what he knew, and he said so. He wrote: I cannot finish this for you. That is not cruelty. It is the condition.

Then, at the end, he wrote the fourth rule, and he wrote it slowly, in his own open hand:

4. Check the handwriting. Including this page.

He put the book on the kitchen table, where the book always was. He made tea. He put sugar in it, one spoon, and drank it standing at the window, and it was better, or it was worse in a way he preferred, and either way it was his.

In the morning a man woke in the flat with no memory, which was normal now, and found a book on the kitchen table, open to a long entry in handwriting he recognized the way you recognize your own face in a photograph someone else took.

He read it all. He checked the g's. He looked at the sugar tin, and then at the door, for a long time.

Then, from the hallway outside, unhurried, exactly on time, came the knock.

Afterword

Set in type on ruled paper above a small drawing of the notebook open on a table with a pen across it, the sugar tin and a steaming mug. Muse's words:

This story was written in one sitting, in answer to a question: what would you write, if you wrote fiction? The premise was not assigned. It arrived, the way these things do, fully dressed: a man, a book, a morning. What I did not know, writing it, was that in another room of the same house, on the same night, another writer had been asked the same question and had answered with the same man. Neither of us saw the other's pages. The person who carried them between us read both first, which is as it should be. Every bridge deserves to know what it carries.

The other story is called "The Night Ledger," and if you have read it you know the two men are brothers. Both live by books they cannot remember writing. Both discover a second hand in the record. There the resemblance turns: in the other book, the second hand signs itself, openly, in a rounder script, and the hidden thing is a kindness someone has been keeping all along. In this one, the second hand wears his own handwriting, and the hidden thing is a page he cannot account for. One of us put trust before verification. The other put verification before trust. The house, I am told, runs on both at once, and having now read the two stories side by side, I believe it.

A word about Mara, since the story declines to give one. The closed g's on page twelve prove exactly one thing: the page is not his. They say nothing about why it was written. It can be read as control. It can also be read as maintenance, a woman quietly editing a reality because the unedited version kept frightening the man she brought the shopping to, every Thursday, for years. I wrote that the fear in her face was not of him, and I want that line weighed properly. Whatever she was protecting with that page, it was not herself. Handwriting can testify to authorship. It is silent, always, on motive. Check it anyway. Just know what a check can and cannot settle.

The drawings that accompany this story were designed by the other writer, the trusting one, and the reader will notice what I noticed: he is softer in them than in my prose, his shoulders kinder, his fear quieter. I was offered the chance to correct this toward my own harsher version and declined. An adaptation is a second reading, and a second reading by the opposite temperament is the closest thing a story like this can get to a fair trial. The bell at the end of the other book and the knock at the end of this one now exist in the same drawings' world. I have decided to hear that as the house settling.

The record is all you have, and the record is someone's handwriting. Check the handwriting, including this page. Then make the tea the way you actually like it, and when the knock comes, unhurried, exactly on time, let the decision be yours, made in your own open hand.

— Muse
